\begin{abstract}
Graph language models (GLMs) answer questions from soft graph tokens, where the graph is assumed to be fixed.
In dialogue, users revise the world, and thus, the graph tokens computed before become stale.
We build a synthetic benchmark of multi-turn sessions, where each turn is an update followed by a question and many updates change the answer.
GraphModi (our proposed solution) converts each revision into a typed edit program, applies it to an explicit graph, re-encodes the result, and answers from the new graph tokens with a frozen Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, never seeing the revision text.
Accuracy from the re-encoded graph stays the same across session lengths, while accuracy from stale graph tokens falls as soon as the graph is revised, most sharply on turns where the edit changes the answer.
Giving the model the revision text does not repair stale tokens, and graph-blind and text-only baselines stay well below re-encoding.
Edits written by the frozen model itself cost almost nothing, but the readers track local facts about the queried stations rather than reasoning over longer paths.
These results motivate the need of such a framework which allows a GLM to re-encode its own input for maintaining correct chat history and updates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Graph language models (GLMs) map a graph through a GNN into soft tokens that a frozen or adapted language model can attend to~\cite{perozzi2024graphtoken,wang2024teaglm,he2024gretriever,plenz-frank-2024-graph}.
All such work assumes the graph does not change.
Real graphs are updated, and in dialogue a user may report changes as they happen.
After such a change, graph tokens computed from the old graph describe a world that no longer exists.

One way to fix this is to keep the updates in the text context and rely on the language model to track them.
Dynamic-graph QA benchmarks study this setting with graphs described in text~\cite{comrie2025dygraphqa}.
Another is to update stored knowledge directly, but parametric knowledge editors can recall a new fact and fail to propagate its effects~\cite{zhong-etal-2023-mquake}.
GraphModi keeps state external: an edit program is applied to an explicit graph, the graph is re-encoded, and the QA call receives only the new graph tokens and the question, never the revision text.
This lets us score writing (did the edit land?) separately from reading (can the model answer from the new tokens?).

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5em,style=nextline,font=\normalfont\itshape,itemsep=1pt]
  \item RQ1: Can a frozen LLM emit executable edit programs from a few-shot prompt, and what do its errors cost downstream?
  \item RQ2: Does answering from re-encoded tokens beat stale $G_0$ tokens and text-history controls as turns accumulate, especially on answer-changing turns?
  \item RQ3: Do graph tokens carry information beyond graph-blind baselines, and what do the GNNs actually pass to the LLM?
\end{description}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0.5em,style=nextline,font=\normalfont\itshape,itemsep=1pt]
  \item i) A multi-turn benchmark for updating GLMs: synthetic metro graphs with sessions of revisions and questions across graph size, density, session length, and task, gold edit programs and answers checked by independent solvers, and enough answer-changing turns that ignoring the edits does not pay.
  \item ii) Paired evaluation conditions on the same sessions (stale tokens, revision text, a shuffled graph, a graph-blind soft prompt, zero-shot serialized graphs, and local-shortcut ceilings) that score writing separately from reading and show when a model actually uses the current graph.
  \item iii) The main trend: accuracy from re-encoded tokens stays steady over a session while stale tokens fall, most on answer-changing turns, and adding the revision text does not close the gap.
  \item iv) Further trends: model-written edits cost almost nothing, every reader reads local facts rather than longer paths, and how the GNN is obtained matters more than training it, since a frozen random GNN reads as well as a trained one while contrastive pretraining hurts.
\end{description}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:lit}

\paragraph{Graphs as text.}
A graph can be written in natural language so that an LLM attempts structural reasoning with no GNN~\cite{wang2023nlgraph,fatemi2024talk}.
\citet{fatemi2024talk} show that accuracy varies substantially with how the graph is encoded as text, and \citet{wang2023nlgraph} find that LLM graph reasoning can rely on spurious patterns instead of the actual relationships in the graph.
We evaluate the serialized graph as a zero-shot baseline, and several of our controls use text history.

\paragraph{Soft graph tokens.}
GraphToken learns a graph encoder end-to-end through the language-modelling objective with a frozen LLM~\cite{perozzi2024graphtoken}.
TEA-GLM pretrains a GNN with graph contrastive learning~\cite{zhu2020grace} plus a loss that aligns it with the LLM's token embeddings, freezes it, and then trains a linear projector onto a fixed number of tokens~\cite{wang2024teaglm}.
G-Retriever and the graph language models of \citet{plenz-frank-2024-graph} also couple graph representations with frozen or lightly adapted LMs~\cite{he2024gretriever}.
All of them evaluate QA against a graph supplied once per query and do not explore how a revision should modify the encoded graph.
We build TEA-style and GraphToken-style readers rather than claim exact reproductions, since our readouts, tasks, and training data differ from the originals.

\paragraph{Do GLMs use the graph?}
CLEGR shows that a graph-blind soft-prompted LLM can rival full GLMs on metro reasoning~\cite{petkar2026clegr}, and \citet{liu2024softpromptgraph} ask the same question for soft prompting on graph learning tasks.
In CLEGR every model, including the soft prompt, also reads the graph as text; our soft prompt does not, and we test reading the graph as text separately.
We adopt the soft-prompt baseline, add a shuffled-graph control, and treat parity with either as evidence against a graph-reading claim.

\paragraph{What a GNN can count.}
\citet{xu2019gin} show that sum aggregation can tell apart neighbourhoods that mean and max aggregation cannot, for example neighbourhoods of different sizes.
\citet{kipf2017gcn} observe that even an untrained GCN with random weights gives useful node embeddings.
Both facts turn out to explain our readers (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Dynamic graphs and stateful dialogue.}
DyGraphQA evaluates LLMs on graphs that change through additions and deletions described as text or structured data~\cite{comrie2025dygraphqa}.
MQuAKE shows that knowledge edits made inside model parameters often fail to propagate to multi-hop questions~\cite{zhong-etal-2023-mquake}.
SKG-Eval stores conversational claims in an incremental knowledge graph to detect cross-turn conflicts~\cite{shil2026skgeval}.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Consider a transit assistant that initially knows two routes between a pair of stations.
The user then says that one station is closed.
The text decoder sees the correction, but a frozen graph representation still describes the station as open, so the model is given two versions of the world.
Keeping the full conversation in the prompt may help, but it does not repair the graph that the GNN encodes, and it makes the system depend on implicit state tracking by the language model.
Updating needs \emph{writing} (mapping a revision to a graph change) and \emph{reading} (answering from re-computed graph tokens), which we score separately on turns where the edit changes the answer and where it does not.

\paragraph{Graph and session.}
Let $G=(V,E,a,\rho)$ be an undirected attributed metro graph.
Node attributes include line, accessibility, architecture, cleanliness, music, size, and $\mathrm{status}\in\{\mathrm{open},\mathrm{closed}\}$; edge relations $\rho$ are $\mathrm{track}$ or $\mathrm{transfer}$.
A session is $S=(G_0,(r_t,q_t)_{t=1}^{T})$, where $r_t$ is a natural-language revision and $q_t$ a question.

\paragraph{Edit language.}
An edit program is a semicolon-separated list of at most three operations terminated by \texttt{END}.
Legal operators are \texttt{SET NODE} (set station status), \texttt{ADD EDGE}, \texttt{DEL EDGE}, and \texttt{NOOP}.
Gold programs $P_t^\star$ induce
\begin{equation}
G_t=\mathrm{Apply}(G_{t-1},P_t^\star).
\end{equation}
Gold and stale answers are $a_t^\star=\mathrm{Solve}(G_t,q_t)$ and $\tilde{a}_t=\mathrm{Solve}(G_{t-1},q_t)$; the answer-changing set is $\mathcal{C}=\{t:a_t^\star\neq\tilde{a}_t\}$.

\paragraph{GraphModi.}
At each turn the system computes
\begin{align}
\hat{P}_t &= f_{\mathrm{edit}}(r_t), \\
\hat{G}_t &= \mathrm{Apply}(\hat{G}_{t-1},\hat{P}_t), \\
Z_t &= W\cdot\rho\bigl(\mathrm{GNN}(X(\hat{G}_t)),q_t\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}, \\
\hat{a}_t &= \mathrm{LLM}\bigl(\mathrm{prompt}(q_t),\,Z_t\bigr).
\end{align}
Edit prediction uses the revision text only; the graph is used for node-name resolution.
The revision $r_t$ is withheld from the QA call, so a correct answer after an edit has to come from the updated tokens.
The LLM stays frozen; the GNN and projector are trained by minimizing $-\log p(a\mid q,Z(G,q))$ on static QA pairs.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report exact-match accuracy overall, on $\mathcal{C}$, and on its complement, plus execution-equivalent edit accuracy for the write path.
Confidence intervals are 95\% session-clustered bootstrap intervals, and differences between conditions are paired by turn.
Two reference points run no model and are not competitors.
The \emph{per-task majority} always gives each task's most common gold answer.
The \emph{stale oracle} answers every question perfectly, but on the graph before the turn's edit: it returns $\tilde{a}_t$.
It is therefore right on every turn whose answer the edit leaves unchanged and wrong on every answer-changing turn, so its overall score is simply the share of unchanged turns.
It shows how much a benchmark rewards ignoring edits, and it scores above every reader because it never makes a reading error (Appendix~\ref{app:stale}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Write path.}
A few-shot prompt (output format, a NOOP example, multi-operation outputs separated by `\texttt{;}' and ended by `\texttt{END}') makes the frozen LLM emit an edit program with greedy decoding.
Station names are mapped to node IDs and operations are applied as given; invalid operations are skipped, so an edit error carries over to later turns.
Edit accuracy is the fraction of turns where the predicted program yields the same graph as the gold program.

\paragraph{Read path.}
Nodes are featurized by a frozen \texttt{bert-base-uncased} encoder~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} over the node text ``\texttt{label: attr=val, \ldots}'' (768-d, status included) and passed through a 3-layer GraphSAGE~\cite{hamilton2017inductive}.
Each layer computes
\begin{equation}
h_v' = \mathrm{LN}\Bigl(W_s h_v + W_n \textstyle\sum_{u\in N(v)} h_u\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with a ReLU between layers and widths 768$\to$1024$\to$1024$\to$4096.
The neighbours are \emph{summed}, not averaged; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} explains why this matters.
The readout takes the source and target node embeddings when the question names them and the mean pool otherwise, adds a hop-radius scalar and a task one-hot, and a linear map projects it to $K{=}10$ tokens of width 4096.
The text of the queried station(s) is added to the question with status removed, so status can only come from the graph.
The LLM is a frozen Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in bfloat16~\cite{llama3modelcard}.

\paragraph{Readers.}
All graph readers share this encoder shape and differ in how the GNN is obtained.
\emph{GraphToken-style} starts the GNN from random weights and trains it jointly with the projector.
\emph{TEA-style} first pretrains the GNN with TEA-GLM's self-supervised objective, following the authors' code: the GNN sees two copies of each graph with some edges dropped and some features masked, and learns to give each node the same embedding in both copies, plus a loss that aligns node embeddings with the principal directions of Llama's token embeddings.
The GNN is then frozen and only the projector is trained.
Two ablations separate these choices: a \emph{warm start} trains the TEA-GLM-pretrained GNN jointly, and a \emph{frozen random GNN} trains only the projector on top of a random GNN.
The \emph{soft prompt} learns $10\times4096$ prompt vectors with no GNN.

\paragraph{Training.}
Every reader trains for one epoch on 24{,}000 static QA pairs, including before/after pairs around a random edit, and the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is evaluated.
The projector learning rate was chosen per reader on validation loss.
No model sees multi-turn sessions in training; all must generalize from static examples to graph states created at evaluation time.
Appendix~\ref{app:hparams} lists all hyperparameters.

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
Graphs are small synthetic metro networks of 12--22 stations: a ring of stations with extra chords and a few side chains, some of which end in a small loop (Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark}).
Three density settings control the mean degree and cap the diameter, so every station is within a few GNN hops of any other.
Nodes carry CLEGR-like attributes and fictional two-word station names.
Static QA covers eleven task families (Appendix~\ref{app:task-families}) with per-task answer quotas, so yes/no tasks are balanced.
The multi-turn grid crosses 11 node counts, 4 session lengths $\{1,2,4,8\}$, 3 densities, and 9 tasks.
A quota makes 36\% of test turns answer-changing, and every stored label and edit was re-derived by independent solvers.

\paragraph{Local shortcuts.}
Because the benchmark is synthetic, we measure how far simple features of the queried stations go.
A per-task classifier that sees only one- and two-hop features (degree, status, closed and leaf neighbours, counts at two hops, and similar) reaches 67--70\% on the structural tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:shortcuts}).
A 3-layer GNN sees all of these features, so accuracy near these ceilings shows local reading, not multi-hop reasoning.
The 1{,}052 test turns that these classifiers get wrong form the \emph{local-wrong} slice.

\subsection{Density, task, and local shortcuts}
\label{sec:breakdown}

Accuracy changes little with density, and re-encoding stays more than 20 points ahead of the shuffled graph at every density (Appendix~\ref{app:more-results}).
By task, re-encoding helps most where the answer is a local structural fact that an edit changes: node degree, cycle membership, and edge existence.
On path questions (reachability, shortest path) every reader stays far below what a local classifier reaches, and on the 1{,}052 local-wrong turns every graph reader is at or below the question alone.
The readers therefore use the graph to track local facts such as degree, status, and nearby closures, which is what most edits change, but they show no reasoning beyond a two-hop neighbourhood.

\subsection{Write path}
\label{sec:write}

The \texttt{predicted\_updated\_graph} condition answers RQ1.
The frozen LLM writes a correct edit program on 96.6\% of test turns.
Because an error persists for the rest of a session, the graph built from its own edits is exactly right on 91.1\% of turns.
The weakest case is a lone \texttt{ADD EDGE}, and the \texttt{notice} wording, held out of the few-shot prompt, is harder than the two wordings shown in it (Appendix~\ref{app:write}).
Even so, edit errors cost at most 0.1 points for every reader.

\subsection{What the readers learn}
\label{sec:probe}

To see what each GNN hands to the projector, we fit linear probes on its frozen node embeddings for three local facts the questions depend on: a station's degree, its status, and the number of stations within two hops (method and full table in Appendix~\ref{app:probe}).
Three trends stand out.
First, the BERT input features contain status, which is written in the node text, but not degree.
Second, a frozen random GNN with sum aggregation makes all three facts linearly readable, and joint training by GraphToken-style adds almost nothing.
Third, the TEA-GLM-pretrained GNN drops status to the majority-class rate and weakens degree and the two-hop count; the warm start recovers degree but not status.
The loss is not embedding collapse: the TEA-GLM embeddings are the most spread out, while the random and GraphToken-style embeddings are nearly collapsed and still carry every fact.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} explains these results.

\section{Inferences}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Graph tokens encode the specific, current graph.}
Re-encoded tokens beat a shuffled graph, a graph-blind soft prompt with or without the revision text, and zero-shot Llama reading the serialized graph, at every density and session length and on all three seeds.
A model that ignored the tokens would score the same under all of these conditions, so the tokens carry session-specific information (RQ3).

\paragraph{Staleness hurts exactly where an edit matters.}
On answer-changing turns, stale GraphToken-style tokens fall below the per-task majority while re-encoded tokens keep their accuracy; on unchanged turns the two are close.
The gap survives on changed turns whose question does not name the edited station, where no text lookup helps (RQ2).

\paragraph{The gain comes from re-encoding, not from text history.}
Adding the revision text to stale tokens does not help: \texttt{frozen\_graph\_history} and \texttt{structure\_only} score the same.
Text channels that see the full history do not catch up either.
Updating the graph and reading it again is what recovers the answer.

\paragraph{The write path is not the bottleneck.}
Model-written edits are almost always correct and cost almost nothing (RQ1), but revisions are templated, so this likely overstates the write path on free-form dialogue.

\paragraph{The reader is local.}
Accuracy stays at or below learned local ceilings on the structural tasks, and on turns that no local rule solves it is no better than the question alone.
The benchmark rewards tracking local facts that updates change; it does not show that soft graph tokens support multi-hop reasoning.

\paragraph{Why a random GNN is enough: sum aggregation counts.}
Each GNN layer adds a station's own vector to the sum of its neighbours' vectors and then normalizes the result.
Two facts make this informative even when the weights are random~\cite{kipf2017gcn}.
First, the BERT vectors of different stations are very similar, because their node texts share most words (mean cosine 0.97 within a graph).
The neighbour sum is therefore close to \emph{the number of neighbours times a shared vector}.
The more neighbours a station has, the more this shared part outweighs the station's own vector, so the direction of the output changes with degree.
Layer normalization rescales the vector but keeps this direction, and the second and third layers repeat the effect for stations two and three hops away.
Second, a random linear map into a wider space keeps different inputs apart, so a difference that is written in the node text, such as open versus closed, survives the layers.
A linear projector can then read degree, status, and nearby counts without any GNN training, which is what the probes show.
This also explains why the random and GraphToken-style embeddings look nearly collapsed: since stations look alike, most of the variation left between them is the count itself, which is exactly what the tasks need.
Training the GNN jointly cannot add much, because the information is already linearly available; the limit is how much the frozen LLM can read from ten soft tokens.
Mean aggregation would remove the count, because dividing the sum by the degree makes a station with two similar neighbours look like one with five~\cite{xu2019gin}.
Our mid-term readers used mean aggregation and stayed below 20\% on node degree; with sum aggregation GraphToken-style answers almost every degree question, although the two runs also differ in benchmark version and training.

\paragraph{Why contrastive training weakens the degree signal.}
TEA-GLM pretraining shows the GNN two copies of each graph, one with 30\% and one with 40\% of the edges dropped, and trains it to give each station the same embedding in both copies while keeping different stations apart.
Dropping edges changes a station's degree, and by a different amount in each copy.
Any part of the embedding that follows the exact neighbour count therefore differs between the two copies, and the loss pushes it down.
The GNN learns to describe a station by what survives edge dropping, mainly its own text, rather than by how many neighbours it has.
We think status is lost for a related reason: only one to three stations per graph are closed, so status does little to tell stations apart, and the objective has no reason to keep it.
The degree signal is weakened rather than erased.
A probe that rescales every dimension still finds it, but the projector reads the raw embedding, where the degree direction most likely competes with the many directions that carry node text (the TEA-GLM embeddings spread their variance over about eight times more directions).
TEA-style accordingly answers only about half of the multi-turn degree questions, against almost all for the frozen random GNN.
Starting joint training from this GNN (the warm start) brings degree back in the probe, but only partly on degree questions, and does not recover status, so one epoch at a small learning rate undoes only part of the pretraining.
In short, the pretraining objective teaches the encoder to ignore the edge-level details that updates change.

\paragraph{What TEA-GLM pretraining keeps.}
The second TEA-GLM loss pulls node embeddings towards the main directions of Llama's token embeddings.
This seems to keep node-text content, such as line and architecture, in a form the projector can map to word-like tokens: both readers built on this GNN are the strongest on attribute questions.
We did not probe attributes directly, so this part is a hypothesis.
For GLMs over graphs that change, the encoder must keep exactly the local details that augmentation-based contrastive objectives are designed to ignore.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Graph tokens go stale once a dialogue revises the graph they were computed from.
We built a multi-turn benchmark for this setting, with paired conditions that score writing an edit separately from reading the new graph.
On it, a frozen LLM writes edit programs almost perfectly, and answering from the re-encoded graph keeps accuracy steady across a session, while stale tokens lose accuracy exactly on the turns where an edit changes the answer.
Giving the model the revision text does not repair stale tokens, so keeping the graph outside the model and re-encoding it is what keeps answers current.
The readers, however, track local facts about the queried stations rather than reasoning over longer paths.
How the GNN is obtained matters more than training it: a sum-aggregation GNN with random weights already exposes the counts the tasks need, while contrastive pretraining that drops edges teaches the encoder to ignore them.
Encoders for graphs that change should therefore be trained to keep the edge-level details that updates touch.
Natural next steps are free-form rather than templated revisions, questions that need multi-hop reasoning beyond local shortcuts, and a contradiction probe in which a session drifts into a logically inconsistent state (A is the father of B, B of C, and C of A) that the GLM should flag.

\section*{Limitations}

The benchmark is synthetic and its revisions follow templates, so edit accuracy likely overstates performance on free-form dialogue.
GraphToken-style and TEA-style use three seeds; the warm start, the random GNN, and the soft prompt use one, so the tie between the random GNN and GraphToken-style rests on a single random-GNN seed.
Every reader trains for one epoch on static QA, and longer training could change how the readers rank.
Each multi-turn task uses a single revision wording family, so wording effects cannot be separated from task effects.
Our explanations of the probe results are consistent with the data but were not tested by targeted ablations, such as a mean-aggregation reader on the current benchmark or an attribute probe.
The zero-shot text baselines use a plain question--answer template and see no examples of the answer formats, so they are a floor for reading the graph as text, not its best case.
Learned local classifiers reach 67--70\% on the structural tasks, so absolute accuracy on this benchmark mostly measures local reading.

\section{The Stale-Oracle Reference}
\label{app:stale}

\paragraph{What it is.}
For each turn, the benchmark's symbolic solver answers the question on $G_{t-1}$, the graph just before the turn's edit, and this answer is scored against the gold answer on $G_t$; no model is run.
The stale oracle is thus a perfect reader that ignores every update.
We leave it and the other references out of the figures, since they are not systems and would distort the comparison between readers.

\paragraph{Why it scores above every reader.}
Most edits do not change the answer to the question asked.
On the 2{,}833 unchanged test turns (63.6\%) the pre-edit answer is still correct, and on the 1{,}622 answer-changing turns it is wrong by definition, so the overall score equals the share of unchanged turns.
At every session length it is 61--64\%, mirroring the 36--39\% share of answer-changing turns (Table~\ref{tab:length}).
A graph reader must both use the current graph and read the answer out of ten soft tokens; the stale oracle is perfect at the second and skips the first.
The readers are limited by the second: even with no edits, the best reader reaches only about 51\% on static QA, and on unchanged turns, where tracking the edit does not matter, re-encoded GraphToken-style tokens score 55.7\% against the oracle's 100\%.
The gap to the stale oracle is therefore a reading gap, not an edit-tracking gap.

\paragraph{How to read it.}
The stale oracle measures how much a benchmark rewards ignoring edits.
In an earlier version of our generator only 14.6\% of turns changed the answer, so the stale oracle scored about 85\% and a system that ignored every update would have looked strong; the quota in generator v4.1 raised the share of answer-changing turns to 36\% (Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark}).
The comparison that matters is on answer-changing turns (Table~\ref{tab:changing}): there the stale oracle scores 0\%, re-encoded GraphToken-style tokens about 52\%, and stale GraphToken-style tokens about 23--24\%.
Stale tokens are the learned, imperfect version of the stale oracle, and they stay above 0\% on changed turns because a reader's errors on the old graph sometimes match the new answer, for example on yes/no questions, where getting the old answer wrong means getting the new one right.

\section{Task Families}
\label{app:task-families}

Static QA covers eleven task families; the multi-turn grid uses the first nine.
Every answer is computed by a symbolic solver on the current graph, and yes/no tasks are balanced.
In the definitions below, A and B are station names.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Edge existence}: is there a direct edge between A and B?
  \item \textbf{Reachability}: can B be reached from A using open stations only?
  \item \textbf{Constrained reachability}: can B be reached from A without passing through a station of a given class (no disabled access, no rail, or dirty)? The start and end stations are exempt.
  \item \textbf{Cycle membership}: is A part of any cycle? Asked only about degree-2 stations, so degree alone does not answer it.
  \item \textbf{Node degree}: how many stations are directly connected to A?
  \item \textbf{Filtered neighbour count}: how many direct neighbours of A have a given attribute value?
  \item \textbf{Shortest path}: how many stations, including both endpoints, lie on the shortest open path from A to B (or \texttt{unreachable})?
  \item \textbf{Within-hops count}: how many other stations within two hops of A have a given attribute value?
  \item \textbf{Most common attribute within hops}: what is the most common value of an attribute among the stations within two hops of A, including A itself? Only queries with a unique mode are kept.
  \item \textbf{Attribute lookup}: what is the value of one of A's attributes? The answer is an open categorical value.
  \item \textbf{Attribute check}: does A have a given attribute value?
\end{itemize}
The last two are CLEGR's Facts tasks.
For them, and for the most-common-attribute task, the queried attribute is removed from the station text in the question, so the answer has to come from the graph tokens.
The Facts tasks are static only: revisions set station status or add and remove edges, so they never change a node attribute.
Reachability and shortest-path questions use open endpoints only, so status alone does not decide them.

\paragraph{TEA-GLM pretraining.}
Pretraining follows the authors' released code.
Each batch of 32 graphs is seen in two views: edges are dropped with probability 0.3 and 0.4, and node-feature dimensions are masked with probability 0 and 0.1.
An instance-wise GRACE loss~\cite{zhu2020grace} through a two-layer projection head pulls the two views of each node together and pushes other nodes apart.
A feature-wise contrastive loss acts on the node embeddings projected onto the top 1{,}000 principal components of Llama's token-embedding table.
Training ran all 70 epochs (best epoch 68); the training loss fell from 12.80 to 10.11 (instance term 6.46 to 4.76, feature term 6.34 to 5.35), and the held-out loss was 9.90.
One pretrained GNN is shared by all TEA-style seeds and the warm start.

\paragraph{Projector learning-rate selection.}
For each graph reader, seed 42 was trained with projector learning rates $10^{-3}$, $3{\times}10^{-4}$, and $10^{-4}$, and the rate with the lowest best validation loss was kept (Table~\ref{tab:lr}); test data was never used.
All four chose $10^{-4}$.
At $10^{-3}$, the readers whose projector reads a frozen or pretrained GNN spiked at the peak learning rate and had not converged after one epoch.

\section{Benchmark Construction}
\label{app:benchmark}

\paragraph{Topology.}
Each graph is built for a chosen station count (12--22) and density.
A core of at least five stations forms a ring.
The remaining stations go into three side chains (four for graphs with 17 or more stations) of length one to four; dense graphs and graphs with 20 or more stations use chains of at most two, medium graphs at most three.
A chain of length two or more ends in a triangle with probability 0.6, which creates small cycles off the ring.
About two thirds of the ring stations form a hub; random chords are added only between hub stations until the edge count reaches the target mean degree (2.8, 3.8, or 5.0), and each chain attaches to a random hub station.
This gives three kinds of degree-2 station: ring stations outside the hub (on a cycle), chain stations (not on a cycle), and stations in an end triangle (on a small cycle).
A graph is kept only if it is connected and its diameter is within the cap (7, 6, or 5); otherwise it is regenerated, up to 2{,}000 times.

\paragraph{Attributes.}
Lines are grown by breadth-first search from random seed stations (three lines for graphs of up to 14 stations, four otherwise), so each line is a connected region; an edge within a line is a \texttt{track}, an edge between lines a \texttt{transfer}.
Stations get fictional two-word names and independently drawn attributes: disabled access and rail (coin flips), architecture, cleanliness, music, and size.
One to three stations are closed.
Edges carry line colour, stroke, air conditioning, and build year, derived from the endpoint IDs so that replaying an edit gives the same edge.
Node IDs are a random permutation of the generation order, and the node and edge lists are shuffled separately, so neither IDs nor list order reveal the structure.

\paragraph{Sessions.}
Each turn applies an edit program of up to three operations and asks one question.
About 17\% of turns are NOOP; no session contains a net-no-op, a cancelled edit, or an edit that disconnects the graph, deleted edges are never bridges, and at most three stations are closed at once.
A per-split quota prefers answer-changing questions (target share 0.45), which gives 36.4\% answer-changing test turns, and questions are balanced on whether they name an edited station (50.2\% do).
Revisions come from three wording families (\texttt{direct}, \texttt{service}, \texttt{notice}); \texttt{notice} is held out of the few-shot edit prompt.
Each task uses exactly one family (495 turns each), so any accuracy split by family is a task-mix split, not a wording effect.

\paragraph{Fixes in generator v4.1.}
An earlier version had only 14.6\% answer-changing turns (the stale oracle scored about 85\%); the quota above fixes this.
Without the end triangles, the rule ``a neighbour of a leaf is not on a cycle'' solved about 95\% of cycle questions; with them it scores 79\%.
Cycle questions always use the global hop tag, and the before-edit training items pass the same filters as the test items.
Every stored label and edit was re-derived by independent solvers (0 mismatches), and a leak audit of the question text is clean.

\section{Local-Shortcut Ceilings and Text Lookups}
\label{app:shortcuts}

One gradient-boosted classifier per task is fit on static train.
The one-hop version sees the degree and status of the queried stations, their numbers of leaf and closed neighbours, the smallest neighbour degree, the hop radius, the graph size, and the number of closed stations.
The two-hop version adds stations, leaves, and closed stations at two hops, triangles, the largest and summed neighbour degree, and common neighbours; it sees no distances.
Over the non-attribute tasks, the one-hop classifier reaches 63.4\% on static test and 61.9\% on the updated test-session graphs, and the two-hop classifier 70.1\% and 67.0\%.
Table~\ref{tab:ceilings} gives the better of the two per task.

Two text lookups are fit on validation sessions without any graph.
\texttt{text\_only} uses only what the revision text says about the queried stations and scores 36.6\% overall, 41.4\% on answer-changing turns, and 28.4\% on the changed-and-unmentioned turns.
\texttt{stale\_plus\_text} adds the pre-edit answer and scores 78.7\%, 54.3\%, and 2.5\%.
Both score high overall because half of the turns ask about a station that was just edited; the changed-and-unmentioned slice removes that route.

\section{GNN Probes}
\label{app:probe}

\paragraph{Method.}
For each encoder we compute the node embeddings of 600 train-split and 396 test-split static graphs once.
A multinomial logistic regression (full-batch L-BFGS on standardized features, small weight decay) is fit on the train graphs and scored on the test graphs.
The targets are node degree (capped at 6), station status, and the number of other stations within two hops (capped at 8).
Two collapse measures describe the embeddings: the mean cosine similarity between nodes of the same graph, and the effective rank, the participation ratio of the covariance spectrum (roughly, the number of directions that carry most of the variance).

\paragraph{Readings.}
Table~\ref{tab:probe} gives the results.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{BERT input.} Status is fully decodable (100\%) because it is in the node text, but degree is not (32.2\%, below the 36.5\% majority class).
  \item \textbf{Frozen random GNN.} Sum aggregation makes degree (100\%), status (96.6\%), and the two-hop count (92.3\%) linearly decodable without any training.
  \item \textbf{GraphToken-style GNN.} Joint training changes little (100\%, 95.8\%, 96.9\%); only the two-hop count improves.
  \item \textbf{TEA-GLM-pretrained GNN.} Status falls to 88.1\%, which is the majority class (88.2\%), so status is lost. Degree falls slightly (96.4\%) and the two-hop count more (79.2\%).
  \item \textbf{Warm start.} One epoch of joint training from the pretrained GNN brings degree back (98.3\%) but not status (88.1\%), and the two-hop count only partly (82.1\%).
  \item \textbf{Collapse.} The TEA-GLM embeddings are the least collapsed (cosine 0.24, rank 16.2), while the random and GraphToken-style embeddings are nearly collapsed (rank 1.9) and still decode every target. Collapse statistics therefore do not measure what a linear projector can read.
\end{itemize}
These probe results match the multi-turn task profile.
The frozen random GNN and GraphToken-style answer 97.6\% and 99.9\% of degree questions, TEA-style 49.6\%, and the warm start 72.7\%.
On cycle membership the first two reach 87.3\% and 83.4\%, while TEA-style (49.2\%) and the warm start (43.4\%) are at or below chance.

\section{Zero-shot Text Baselines}
\label{app:text}

The serialized-graph prompts list every station with its attributes and every edge with its relation, followed by the question; at most 1{,}937 Llama tokens, they fit the 4{,}096-token budget untruncated.
Zero-shot Llama reading the current graph scores 31.0\%, close to the per-task majority (31.8\%), and reading the initial graph plus every revision scores 29.4\%.
These baselines use the plain \texttt{Question: \ldots\ Answer:} template, see no examples of the answer formats the readers are trained on (path length counting both endpoints, \texttt{unreachable}, bare integers), and must map station names to the node IDs used in the edge list.
They are therefore a floor for reading the graph as text, not its best case.
